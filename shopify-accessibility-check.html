How to check if your Shopify store is accessible: a 20-minute self-check

You don't need to be a developer to find the most common accessibility problems on a Shopify store. This guide walks you through free tools, the issues they usually find, where to fix each one in Shopify, and the two quick manual tests that no tool can do for you.

Important: this guide helps you find and fix common barriers. It is not legal advice, and passing these checks does not make a store "ADA compliant". For legal certainty, talk to a qualified accessibility auditor or a lawyer.

Why it's worth 20 minutes

Accessibility problems lose you customers: people with low vision, older shoppers, keyboard users and anyone reading on a phone in bright light. They are also a legal risk in the US. The law firm Seyfarth Shaw counted 5,006 federal ADA Title III lawsuits in the first half of 2026, and website cases are a growing share of them. UsableNet, which tracks these cases, reports that most website and app lawsuits target companies with less than $50 million in yearly revenue (KRDO), and that many lawsuits name stores that already had an accessibility widget installed.

Step 1 · Run a free automated check (5 minutes)

Pick one page of each type: your home page, a collection, a product page, the cart and your contact page. Shopify themes reuse the same templates, so one page of each type shows most problems. Then use any of these free tools:

  • WAVE by WebAIM: paste your page address at wave.webaim.org.
  • Lighthouse, built into Chrome: right-click the page › Inspect › Lighthouse › tick Accessibility › Analyze page load.
  • axe DevTools, a free browser extension from Deque.

Automated tools are a good first pass. In a large study, Deque found that automated tests caught about 57% of the accessibility issues found in full audits. The rest need a human, which is why steps 3 and 4 matter.

Step 2 · Fix the 6 issues tools find most often on Shopify stores

1. Images without a text description (alt text)

What it means for shoppers: screen-reader users hear nothing, or a file name, so they can't tell which product they're looking at.

Fix in Shopify: Products › open a product › click the image › Add alt text. Describe what matters to a buyer ("Brown leather tote bag, front view"). Decorative images can stay empty. For images placed in theme sections, set the alt text where your theme stores the image (often Content › Files).

2. Text that is too light (color contrast)

What it means: pale grey prices, white text on light buttons and faint footer links are hard to read for many people.

Fix: Online Store › Themes › Customize › Theme settings › Colors. Normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 with its background (3:1 for large text). WebAIM's free contrast checker tells you whether a color pair passes.

3. Icon links and buttons with no name

What it means: the cart, search or social icons are announced as just "link" or "button".

Fix: most of these live in your theme's header and footer, so one edit fixes every page. The link needs a text label, for example aria-label="Cart". If you don't edit code, ask your theme developer for this specific change.

4. Form fields without labels

What it means: quantity boxes, discount codes and newsletter sign-ups are announced without saying what to type. Fields labelled only by greyed-out placeholder text are a problem too, because the hint disappears as soon as someone types.

Fix: every field needs a real label. Many themes include a hidden "Quantity" label; check it hasn't been removed by an app or customization.

5. Headings that skip levels

What it means: screen-reader users navigate by headings; jumping from a level-2 to a level-4 heading makes them think they missed a section.

Fix: keep one main heading per page and use levels in order. Change how a heading looks with your theme's style settings, not by picking a smaller heading level.

6. Embedded content without a title

What it means: maps, videos and some app widgets load inside a frame that screen readers announce without saying what it contains.

Fix: give each frame a short title ("Map to our shop"). For app widgets, ask the app developer.

Step 3 · The 5-minute keyboard test

Put your mouse aside and use only Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space and the arrow keys:

  • Can you see where you are at every step (a visible outline around the focused item)?
  • Can you open the menu, choose a product variant, add it to the cart and reach checkout?
  • Can you close pop-ups (newsletter, cookie banner) with the keyboard, and does focus never get stuck?

If any step fails, note the page and the element: that's a high-priority fix, because it blocks some customers from buying at all.

Step 4 · A quick screen-reader check

On a Mac, press Cmd+F5 to turn VoiceOver on and off. On Windows, the free screen reader NVDA takes a few minutes to install. Listen to your product page: is the product name announced, the price, the variant choice, the "Add to cart" button? You don't need to master the tool to notice when something is silent or confusing.

What about accessibility widgets and overlays?

Widgets that add a toolbar to your store don't fix the underlying code, and lawsuits have named stores that already used them. Fixing the actual issues in your theme is the reliable route.

When to get professional help

If you've received a demand letter, sell to the public sector, or your checks turned up many keyboard or screen-reader problems, a full manual audit by a qualified accessibility professional is worth it.
